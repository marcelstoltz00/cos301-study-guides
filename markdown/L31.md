## Scope

**General study guide — no L31 lecture slides were supplied.** This guide focuses on explaining and defending a software engineering project. Suggested timings are practice examples, not an official presentation rubric. Check any course-specific instructions separately.

## 1. Start with the audience and outcome

A presentation is successful when the audience can understand the relevant problem, assess the evidence and act on the conclusion. Start by writing one sentence describing what they should know, believe or decide by the end.

Identify their background and concerns. A client may care about a completed workflow and its benefit; an engineering examiner may ask how the design meets requirements and what evidence supports quality claims. A mixed audience needs a plain-language overview before technical depth. Define specialised terms at first use and avoid assuming everyone knows your project vocabulary.

## 2. Build a reasoned narrative

A useful structure is **problem → constraints → approach → evidence → limitations → next step**. State the user problem before showing implementation details. Explain a design choice through the requirement it addresses, alternatives considered and the resulting trade-off.

Weak: “We used a queue because it is modern.” Stronger: “Payment processing may pause; a durable queue lets us accept pending orders while workers recover. We must now handle duplicates and communicate pending status.” The second explanation connects the mechanism to a need and acknowledges its cost.

| Part | Audience question |
|---|---|
| Problem | Who is affected and why does it matter? |
| Requirements | What must the solution achieve? |
| Approach | Why was this design chosen? |
| Demonstration | Can we see the important workflow? |
| Evidence | What supports the claimed behaviour and quality? |
| Limitations | What remains incomplete or uncertain? |
| Next step | What decision or action follows? |

## 3. Claims need appropriate evidence

A screenshot can show that a screen exists; it does not prove its server-side permissions. A unit-test count does not establish production capacity. Match each claim to an observation that can support it.

For a performance claim, report the workload, environment, sample or duration, metric and result. “p95 response time was 240 ms during a ten-minute run at 100 requests/second on the stated test environment” is assessable. “The system is very fast” is not. Do not extrapolate a small test to every production condition.

Distinguish **implemented**, **tested**, **demonstrated**, **planned** and **assumed**. If presenting sample data or a recorded demo, label it. Attribute external diagrams, code and results so original work and borrowed material are clear.

## 4. Make visuals carry one clear message

Use readable text, sufficient contrast and direct labels. A slide title can state its conclusion, such as “Two app replicas still share one database failure point.” Include only the detail needed for that conclusion.

Use a diagram for structure or flows, a chart for a measured comparison, and a short table for trade-offs. Explain arrow direction and notation; do not present a whole unreadable architecture poster. Highlight the part currently being discussed.

Avoid colour as the only signal: combine it with text, symbols or line styles. Include units, axes and baselines in charts. A truncated axis can exaggerate differences; if necessary for a particular analysis, make the scale explicit and explain it. Cite the source of external data.

For code, show a small relevant excerpt and explain its purpose or invariant. Reading an entire source file aloud rarely helps the audience understand the design.

## 5. A demo is a planned experiment

Choose a user journey that demonstrates a central requirement. State the starting condition, action and expected outcome. Prepare realistic, non-sensitive sample data and reset the application to a known state before rehearsal and delivery.

Include one useful negative case when time permits, such as a rejected unauthorised request. Explain what it proves and what it does not. Keep credentials and personal data out of projected screens and logs.

Prepare a fallback: a clearly labelled recording, screenshots or a walkthrough of expected results. If the live demo fails, acknowledge the failure, attempt a bounded recovery and switch to the fallback. Do not spend all remaining time debugging or represent recorded output as live evidence.

## 6. Timing and rehearsal

An illustrative ten-minute plan is one minute for the problem, one for requirements, two for design, three for the demo, two for evidence and limitations, and one for the conclusion. Adapt to the actual rubric and whether questions are included in the allocation.

Rehearse aloud with the real slides, device and demo state. Measure transitions and setup time as well as speaking time. Identify optional detail to cut if running late; protect the core conclusion and evidence. Appendix slides can hold deeper diagrams and results for questions.

A useful rehearsal review asks: Where did the listener lose the thread? Which claim lacked evidence? Which term needed explanation? Improve those specific issues rather than only repeating the same script.

## 7. Delivery and team coordination

Speak at a pace that leaves time to interpret diagrams. Use pauses at transitions and signpost connections: “This requirement explains the next design choice.” Face the audience, keep pointing purposeful and avoid reading slide paragraphs verbatim.

Assign clear speaking roles and handoffs in team presentations. Everyone should understand the overall system even when specialising in one section. Rehearse the laptop or slide-control handoff. Avoid inconsistent terminology and repeated introductions that consume the available time.

## 8. Questions and honest uncertainty

Listen to the whole question, clarify ambiguity if needed, answer the central point first, then provide evidence. If a premise is incorrect, explain the relevant distinction respectfully. Stay within what you can substantiate.

If you do not know, say what is known and how you would verify the missing part. “We measured single-zone failure recovery; we have not tested a regional outage” is more useful than inventing a number. Record follow-up commitments only when you can fulfil them.

## 9. Worked example: defend a queue

**Claim:** orders can remain pending during a temporary payment-worker outage. **Design:** durable queue plus workers. **Demo:** stop a worker, submit an order, show pending state, restart and observe completion. **Evidence:** queue depth, processing logs and one recorded payment effect per order ID. **Limitation:** the result depends on queue capacity, retention and correct duplicate handling. **Next step:** test longer outages and capacity limits.

This connects requirement, mechanism and evidence without claiming that one demonstration proves every reliability property.
